# §6.3 — Deep dive: what stayed out of the chapter's body

> [`rajada-nasdaq.c`](medicoes/rajada-nasdaq.c) — the chapter publishes the main
> line of the argument, and this file keeps the rest.
>
> **Why it exists.** §6.3 answers a single question: *why the average rate does
> not size an RX ring*. The investigation behind that answer went well beyond it
> — socket buffers, RSS, and a second documentary anchor in Nasdaq's FAQ.
> Publishing all of it on the main line would turn a fundamentals section into a
> study of market-data receive engineering. Keeping it out of the body, without
> deleting it, is the separation between **the instrument's complexity** and
> **the lesson's complexity**.

## 1. What the source says, and what it does not

Two publications, with the exact column for anyone who wants to check. In the
[bandwidth report][nasdaqbw], the *NASDAQ TotalView-ITCH 4.1* row carries **61 Mb** under
*Standard Bandwidth Recommendation – Current* and **67 Mb** under *– New*, effective
10/1/2010. And the FPGA product specification says:

> *"Given the unshaped network traffic, NASDAQ OMX is requiring firms to have 10 Gb or 40 Gb
> network connection into the Carteret, NJ data center."*
> — [TotalView-ITCH FPGA 4.1][nasdaqfpga], *Overview*

**Sixty-seven megabits of recommendation, on a required ten-gigabit link: a factor of 149.**
But what the source does *not* say changes how that factor may be used, and it is worth
stating before any table:

> **Three caveats.**
>
> **1. 67 Mb is a bandwidth recommendation, not a measured average.** The document does not
> state the window the value was computed over. Using it as a long-run mean is a **modelling
> choice**, not a reading of the source.
>
> The consequence, stated as conditional analysis: **if the 67 Mb sits above the real
> long-run mean**, the model's duty cycle falls, and the bursts needed to preserve the same
> link-limit scenario become **more concentrated** than the ones simulated here. None of that
> attributes to Nasdaq — or to market practice — anything the cited documentation does not
> establish.
>
> **2. The 10 Gb link is a connection requirement, not an observed rate.** That the feed is
> *unshaped* does not prove it saturates 10 Gb/s. Here, 10 Gb/s is the **link-limit
> scenario**: the worst case the infrastructure allows. The specification only states that the
> pattern *"reflects true market volumes at all points during the trading day"*.
>
> **3. And Nasdaq publishes no bandwidth recommendation for the FPGA product.** The product
> FAQ is explicit: *"Nasdaq does not have a specific bandwidth recommendation for the
> TotalView-ITCH FPGA product"* ([FAQ][nasdaqfaq]). The 67 Mb belong to the **software**
> product. Applying them to the FPGA — which is precisely the unshaped feed — is one more
> modelling choice, not a reading of the source.
>
> What the FAQ publishes instead is guidance in **messages**, and it becomes scenario B
> further down: *"Nasdaq would simply advise TotalView-ITCH FPGA firms to be prepared to
> handle milli-second data bursts of up to 2,000 messages."*

## 2. `SO_RCVBUF`: the queue you ask for is not the queue you have

The chapter's body shows the socket losing more at the same depth. What it does
not detail is **how that depth is measured**, and the result is the most
operational finding of this investigation.

The descriptor ring is not the only queue on the receive path — and on the **conventional**
path it is not even the deciding one. After it comes the socket receive buffer, and that is
where an ordinary UDP receiver's drops usually show up: as `UdpRcvbufErrors` in `netstat -su`.

```
  SO_RCVBUF     granted   datagrams  assumed   ratio    recv()  disp     recvmmsg(32)  disp
  ---------  ----------  ----------  -------  -----  --------  ----  --------------  ----
     212992      425984         443     1664   3.8x    229 ns  33.6%         200 ns  19.1%
    1048576     2097152        2184     8192   3.8x    266 ns  7.5%         212 ns  7.6%
    8388608     8388608        8738    32768   3.8x    277 ns  30.3%         205 ns  2.7%
   33554432     8388608        8738    32768   3.8x    276 ns  34.6%         206 ns  4.3%

  The SAME burst, drained by the socket instead of the ring (queue = 8738 datagrams):

  ring(n)      offered     dropped      loss   occ.max  median(us)    p99(us)
  -------      -------     -------      ----   -------  ----------    -------
     8738     15635809      253842    1.623%      8738         1.8     8356.5
     8738     15635809      225244    1.441%      8738         1.8     7721.3

     row 1: recv() one by one    -> 1009 ns per packet (732 of work + 277 of socket)
     row 2: recvmmsg in batch    -> 937 ns per packet (732 of work + 205 of socket)
     drain: 991159 and 1067481 packets/s, against 1366272 for the pure ring
```

> **Read the `disp` column before comparing the two calls.** This collection ran with the
> machine in use — by me, repeating the program — and dispersion ranged from 8% to 106%. With
> the machine idle the same collection gives 1.5% to 4.8%. `recvmmsg`'s advantage holds as a
> **direction**, which repeats, not as a value.


**Asking for 8 MB for 256 B datagrams, the naive arithmetic over the value
`getsockopt` returns promises 32,768 and the machine delivers 8,738** — a factor
of **3.8×**, stable across the whole range. At the default it is **443
datagrams**, which at 3.9 million packets per second is **0.1 ms of burst**. And
asking for 32 MB returns 8 MB silently: the kernel caps at `net.core.rmem_max`
with no error at all.

> **Where the 3.8× comes from, and what this experiment does not separate.** Two
> things go in: Linux **doubles** the value passed to `setsockopt` for internal
> accounting, and `getsockopt` returns the doubled value — so the naive
> arithmetic already starts from an inflated number; and each datagram consumes
> receive structures beyond its payload. The program measures the **aggregate
> effect**; it does not decompose how much belongs to each part. The operational
> lesson does not depend on the decomposition: **check with `getsockopt` and
> measure how many datagrams fit**, instead of dividing bytes by payload.

## 3. RSS does not divide what is a single flow

The tables above have **one queue**. The obvious way out would be to spread the feed across
several with [RSS][scaling] — and it does not work here, for a reason the source itself
supplies.

RSS distributes by **header hash**. A multicast feed is *one flow*: same source IP, same
destination group, same ports. What divides a feed is not the subscriber's NIC — it is the
exchange, when it publishes the feed on several channels. And the [FAQ][nasdaqfaq] answers
this with no room left:

> *"Is Nasdaq planning to offer the FPGA feed in a multi-channel (multi-thread) option?
> **No.** For the initial implementation, Nasdaq will offer only a single channel version of
> the Nasdaq TotalView-ITCH FPGA."*

```
  channels  queues  ideal div.  burst rho      loss  p99(us)   note
  --------  ------  ----------  ---------  --------  -------  -----------------------------------
         1       1           1       2.86   21.763%    755.9  TotalView-ITCH 4.1, one queue
         1       4           1       2.86   21.763%    755.9  the SAME feed with 4 RSS queues
         1      16           1       2.86   21.763%    755.9  and with 16 queues
         3       3           3       0.95    0.000%     28.3  the SAME traffic split into 3 (ideal)
         8       8           8       0.36    0.000%      2.1  the SAME traffic split into 8 (ideal)
```

**The first three rows are identical.** Sixteen queues do not help a single-channel feed. The
qualifier *"for the initial implementation"* is the source's and it stays: it does not support
"TotalView-ITCH FPGA is always single-channel".

> **Two caveats about what this table is not.** `min(channels, queues)` is the model's
> **ideal** divisor, not the effective one: RSS maps by hash, and three flows across three
> queues can collide into two — the real divisor has to be **measured on the card's RSS**, and
> here it is the ceiling. And the 3- and 8-channel rows **do not simulate** Level 2 or
> TotalView-Aggregated: they are the *same* traffic from this scenario, ideally partitioned.
> Those products have rates of their own.

## 4. External validation: messages are not descriptors

The tables above are **scenario A — the link limit**: how much fits in 10 Gb/s. The FAQ gives
an independent anchor, and in another unit:

> *"Nasdaq would simply advise TotalView-ITCH FPGA firms to be prepared to handle milli-second
> data bursts of up to 2,000 messages."* — [TotalView-ITCH FPGA FAQ][nasdaqfaq]

**The sentence speaks of messages; the ring counts descriptors.** And it does not say "2,000
messages in exactly 1 ms" — converting straight to 2 Mpps would add a temporal precision the
source does not give.

> **Three things here are assumptions, not source**, and the result depends on all three: the
> **1 ms window** (the FAQ says only "milli-second data bursts"), the **40 B per ITCH message**
> (size varies by message type) and the **packing**, which is the table's axis precisely
> because it is what changes the result most. The table is a sensitivity analysis, not a
> reconstruction of the packing Nasdaq actually uses.

```
  msgs/datagram  payload(B)  on wire(B)  datagrams  implied Gb/s  drained/ms  excess
  -------------  ----------  ----------  ---------  ------------  ----------  ------
              1          40         106       2000          1.70        1365     635
              2          80         146       1000          1.17        1365       0
              4         160         226        500          0.90        1365       0
              8         320         386        250          0.77        1365       0
             16         640         706        125          0.71        1365       0
             32        1280        1346         63          0.68        1365       0
             64        2560        2626         32          0.67        1365       0  <- over 1500 B
```

**Three different rates on the same line**, and confusing them is the classic RX-sizing
mistake: **messages** is what the source publishes, **datagrams** is what the ring counts,
**bits** is what the link carries. `2,000 messages ≠ 2,000 datagrams ≠ 2,000 descriptors` —
MoldUDP64's packing density decides, and that is an assumption, not a source.

> **On the 1,500 B ceiling: the source does not state the layer.** The FAQ says *"packets up
> to 1,500 bytes in length"* without specifying whether it counts the Ethernet frame, the IP
> packet or the UDP payload. What can be said safely is the extreme: `32 × 40 = 1,280 B` is
> below the limit under any reading, and `64 × 40 = 2,560 B` is over it under any reading.
> Computing the **exact** maximum of messages per packet would require knowing the convention.

#### The two scenarios, in the same quantity

The first version of this section compared different things — it said scenario B "asks for at
most 2,000 descriptors". **It does not.** Two thousand is the number of *messages* offered
and, in the extreme case of one message per datagram, also the number of *datagrams* offered.
What the ring has to absorb is the **excess**, because the consumer drains the whole time —
and the excess was already in the column next to it:

```
  A (link limit, mean 1 ms burst)       : 2,517 descriptors
  B (2,000 msgs in 1 ms, 1 msg/datagram):   635 descriptors
  B with 2 messages per datagram        :     0
```

> **And there is no contradiction between the sources** — the two scenarios answer different
> questions. **A** asks *"what if the 10 Gb/s link were occupied up to that limit?"*. **B**
> asks *"what follows from the documented guidance of up to 2,000 messages in a
> millisecond-scale burst?"*. Nasdaq does not claim the two describe the same event.
>
> Under the assumptions adopted, **the link-limit scenario produces a larger backlog than the
> scenario derived from the operational guidance**. The first is deliberately conservative;
> the second is anchored in the message count the exchange publishes, but still requires
> assumptions to convert messages into datagrams and duration into rate. Saying which of the
> two you are using, and under which assumptions, is what makes the number defensible.

## 5. The five readings, in full

**1. Average utilisation predicts nothing.** Mean `ρ` is **0.019** — the machine is idle 98.1%
of the time. A monitoring dashboard would show a comfortable system, and even so **29.5% of
the packets die** with a 256-descriptor ring.

**2. All that changed was the distribution in time.** The first two tables offer the same 15.6
million packets. Paced, the loss is **zero** and the ring never exceeds **one** occupied
descriptor. In bursts, the same traffic fills nearly every depth tested.

**3. What governs the loss is accumulation, not a queueing formula.** During the burst `ρ` is
**2.86**, above 1 — and in that regime the queue grows at `λ_burst − μ` per unit time, with no
steady state to compute. It is accumulation arithmetic, not stable-queue theory. `ca²` stands
as evidence of the difference in variability between the two scenarios, not as an explanation
of the loss.

**4. There is an arithmetic, and it has a limit.** The excess of one burst is

```
descriptors = (peak_rate − drain_rate) × burst_duration
            = (3,881,988 − 1,365,000) × 1 ms  =  2,517
```

which is why the 4096 ring already brings loss down to 6.4%. But the sojourn is exponential,
and **36.8% of the bursts** — not half — **exceed the mean duration**, in a distribution with
no upper bound. The median sojourn is `ln 2 ≈ 0.693` of the mean. **No fixed depth covers
the tail**, and no finite `K` solves sustained `λ > μ`: a queue absorbs a surge, never
overload.

This is a **stability and transient-overload** argument, not Little's Law.
Little relates concurrency, latency and throughput in a stable system, and that
is how [§4](README.en.md#4-memory-where-performance-is-really-decided) uses it.
Here `λ_burst > μ`, there is no steady state, and what governs is `dQ/dt = λ − μ`
integrated over the burst.

**5. The socket loses on three fronts, and nobody checks the third.**

*It costs more per packet.* Draining one datagram through the socket charges **331 ns** on top
of the application's work, and the drain rate falls from 1,356,024 to **935,507 packets/s**.
`recvmmsg` in batches gives part of that back, by the same amortisation the batching in
[§6.2](README.en.md#62-the-bus-has-a-budget-too) exploits.

*It loses more with the same queue.* The 8 MB socket holds 8,738 datagrams — practically the
same depth as the 8,192 ring — and loses **1.77% against 1.10%**. Same burst, same queue; the
entire difference is the slower drain.

*And the queue you asked for is not the queue you have.* Asking for 8 MB for 256 B datagrams,
the naive arithmetic over the value `getsockopt` returns promises 32,768 and the machine
delivers **8,738** — a factor of **3.8×**, stable across the whole range. At the default it is
**443 datagrams**, which at 3.9 million packets per second is **0.1 ms of burst**. And asking
for 32 MB returns 8 MB silently: the kernel caps at `net.core.rmem_max` with no error at all.

> **Where the 3.8× comes from, and what this experiment does not separate.** Two things go in:
> Linux **doubles** the value passed to `setsockopt` for internal accounting, and `getsockopt`
> returns the doubled value — so the naive arithmetic already starts from an inflated number;
> and each datagram consumes receive structures beyond its payload. The program measures the
> **aggregate effect**; it does not decompose how much belongs to each part. The operational
> lesson does not depend on the decomposition: **check with `getsockopt` and measure how many
> datagrams fit**, instead of dividing bytes by payload.

> **What to do, then.** Not a bigger ring, and — for a single-channel feed — not more queues
> either. Three levers remain, in this order: **a faster consumer**, because the burst `ρ` is
> the only column that moves the result; **subscribing to the version the exchange publishes
> on several channels**, where one exists; and only then **sizing the ring** by the burst
> excess left per channel, at the smallest value that sustains it.
>
> And this is where the two worlds part. On the socket path the levers are indirect:
> `SO_RCVBUF` delivers 3.8 times less queue than it appears to and saturates at `rmem_max`;
> batching exists only through `recvmmsg`, after the kernel has already copied. In the
> data-plane model they are **direct parameters**: ring depth in descriptors, burst size at
> the receive call, and one queue per lcore by construction.
