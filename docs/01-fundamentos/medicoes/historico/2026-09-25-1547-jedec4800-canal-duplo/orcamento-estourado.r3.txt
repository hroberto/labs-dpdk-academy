  How to read this table

  rho = service / budget. Below 1 the consumer keeps up and the queue
  stays practically empty: the observed latency is the service time
  itself. Above 1 the queue fills to capacity and STAYS full -- every
  packet now waits for the whole queue, and latency stops measuring
  service and starts measuring queue depth.

  THREE READINGS, AND THE THIRD IS THE OPERATIONAL ONE

  1. Loss is a step, not a ramp. It stays at 0.0% until rho is close
     to 1 and only then appears. There is no stable regime of
     "slightly overloaded": either the consumer keeps up, or the
     excess is cumulative and the queue never recovers on its own.

  2. The median latency jumps orders of magnitude at the crossing,
     because on the other side it is a different quantity: before it
     is service, after it is wait. A mean-latency plot crossing that
     point does not show the same variable on both sides.

  3. THE TAIL DEGRADES FIRST. Note the rows where loss is still
     0.0% and the median is still in the tens of ns, but the p99 has
     already climbed two orders of magnitude. It is the only warning
     that arrives BEFORE the damage: whoever monitors mean latency and
     mean utilisation sees nothing, because both stay healthy. That is
     why this material publishes percentiles, not means.

  DESIGN CONSEQUENCE: sizing for the average load is not enough.
  What decides survival is the margin over the PEAK, and the indicator
  that warns in time is the high percentile -- not the mean.

  LIMITATIONS OF THIS MEASUREMENT

  Arrival here is PERIODIC (one packet every 67.2 ns exactly). Real
  traffic arrives in bursts, and bursts bring loss forward: at the same
  mean rate, an irregular arrival fills the queue at a lower rho than
  this table shows. These numbers are therefore the OPTIMISTIC case.
  The machine also has no pinned frequency, and the loop itself pays
  two clock reads per packet -- already accounted for in `service`,
  which is measured under saturation precisely for that reason.
